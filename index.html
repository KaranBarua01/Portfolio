Case study 01 · Research before software

Company & GTM Research

The work evolved from finding companies to building a repeatable way to reason about commercial fit.

Surface task

“Find companies in this industry.”

The deeper question

Which companies are structurally more likely to need what the client can make — and why?

Research model

Client capability→Campaign→Industry→Sub-industry→Commodity / Part→Company→Persona→Contact→Commercial outcome

Capability fit

What can the client actually manufacture, supply, or support, and where does that capability sit in a customer's value chain?

Make-vs-buy reasoning

Does the target appear to make the part internally, source it, outsource a process, or depend on a supplier category where the capability fits?

Context and timing

Expansion, localization, sourcing changes, product launches, hiring, supplier pressure, and other signals can change when an otherwise ordinary company becomes relevant.

Evidence discipline

DNP history, prior outcomes, public evidence, research confidence, and contradictions matter. A plausible story is not treated as a fact.

Why should this company need this capability, why might the need exist now, and what evidence would prove or disprove that hypothesis?

Case study 02 · Flagship system

Chuck

What began as workflow automation became a broader exercise in defining business truth, identity, state, data quality, and safe change.

How the problem expanded

Manual workflow→Automation→Explicit business rules→Canonical state→Historical reconciliation→Stable identity→Database-backed processing→Targeted updates→Scale constraints→Health gates

The questions behind the build

Where should truth live when the same event appears in more than one operational source?

What actually makes two records the same person, meeting, workbook, or business event?

Which source owns which fact, and when should newer information supersede older information?

How should historical records coexist with live updates without double-counting or losing corrections?

What happens when data previously marked unusable becomes valid later?

How do you update a very large master dataset without repeatedly treating the entire table as the unit of work?

How do runtime, write, permission, and platform limits change an architecture that looks simple on paper?

How do you add capability without breaking behavior people already rely on?

What changed in the way I approached the system

I stopped treating automation as “make the computer perform the manual steps.” The harder work was making business meaning explicit: identities, statuses, precedence, eligibility, mappings, recoverable states, historical truth, and the gates that decide whether a system is healthy enough to act.

Preserve what is already healthy. Find the smallest responsible layer. Change that layer without destabilizing the rest of the system.

Public description is intentionally generalized. Internal company, customer, employee, and implementation details are not published.

Focused proof · Data architecture

Think in identities and decisions, not just rows.

Large operational datasets change the question from “How do I update this sheet?” to “How do I locate the smallest set of records that could possibly need work?”

Staging→Identity / index layer→Targeted lookup→Update / insert decision→Master record

Identity before comparison

Decide what uniquely represents a person or record, normalize it, and make that identity the entry point for later decisions.

State can recover

“Unusable” is not always permanent. Newer verified information may need to clear an older failure without erasing the history that explains it.

Process only what changed

Staging, targeted lookups, bounded batches, deduplication, and appearances/history reduce the need for unnecessary full-table work.

Case study 03 · Research intelligence

PYXS

A manufacturing research system built around a harder question than “What does this company do?” — why might an opportunity exist, and what evidence would justify acting on it?

Reasoning chain

Company identity→Facilities→Equipment→Capabilities→Applications→Market context→Buying signals→Timing→Company→Relevant persona

Evidence discipline

Fact→Pattern→Inference→Hypothesis→Evidence needed→Action

The system design keeps identity, facilities, and equipment as explicit records and preserves unknown values rather than filling gaps with convenient assumptions. That makes later reasoning auditable instead of turning inference into fake certainty.

Unknown facts should remain unknown until evidence supports them.

Where the idea goes next

Research should not disappear after a report is sent. Prior research, outcomes, RFQs, signal history, and corrections can become feedback for future decisions — so repeated work compounds into a better model instead of resetting to zero.

Ways I work across a problem

Research decomposition

Turn broad questions into evidence categories, hypotheses, exclusion rules, and repeatable research paths.

Systems modeling

Commercial reasoning

Data modeling

Define identity, ownership, precedence, deduplication, history, and the rules that make operational data trustworthy.

Constraint-first design

Use budgets, permissions, platform limits, processing caps, missing data, and operational risk as design inputs.

Root-cause analysis

Find the smallest responsible layer rather than rewriting stable parts of a system around a symptom.

Product thinking

Ask what information or action will actually help a user decide, not only what the system can technically expose.

Iterative experimentation

Build in checkpoints, verification, edge cases, rollback thinking, and feedback instead of betting everything on a first design.

Business-to-technical translation

Move between operational language and schemas, queries, APIs, automation, tests, and interfaces without losing the business meaning.
